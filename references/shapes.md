# Macro shapes

Load when: diagnosing whole-work structure (plan or manuscript), when the work is non-linear, cyclic, framed, multi-strand or episodic, when chapter/act titles borrow a form from music, dance or ritual, or when a critique is about to assume a rise-and-fall arc.

## How to use

1. Identify the **actual** shape from the text or outline (what the parts really do), then compare with the **intended** shape from the profile. Do this before judging anything else.
2. A work usually combines shapes (a frame around a braid, a spiral inside a countdown). Name the dominant shape and at most two subordinate ones; test each with its own logic gates.
3. A mismatch between intended and actual shape is itself a top-severity finding: most downstream "pacing" or "sagging middle" complaints are symptoms of it.
4. Never measure a non-arc shape against arc norms (midpoint reversal, escalating stakes, climax at 85%). Each shape below states its own engine — what produces forward pull instead.
5. Flags name failure patterns; cite them in findings only with quoted evidence and location.
6. If no shape below fits, describe the pattern in plain words and derive its logic gates from what the work promises in its first movement. Do not force a label.

## Narrative shapes

### Linear arc
- **Recognize by:** one main line of causally linked events rising through complications to a decisive turn and a consequence; time mostly runs forward.
- **Engine:** escalating pressure on a want or value; each step narrows the options.
- **Logic gates:** (1) each major event is caused by the previous ones ("therefore/but", not "and then"); (2) the decisive turn is produced by choices made inside the story, not by chance from outside; (3) the ending answers the question the opening posed, even if the answer is negative.
- **Flags:** `ARC_BY_DEFAULT` — the author did not choose an arc; the material wants another shape and is being forced into rise-and-fall. `AND_THEN_CHAIN` — sequence without causation. `EXTERNAL_RESOLUTION` — the outcome arrives from outside the story's causal chain.
- **Examples:** *Macbeth*; *Madame Bovary* (falling arc); *Crime and Punishment*.
- **Note:** one shape among many, not the default. Use it as the reference only when the profile says so.

### Episodic / picaresque
- **Recognize by:** a sequence of relatively self-contained episodes linked by a traveller, an observer or a situation; episodes could in principle be reordered.
- **Engine:** variety and accumulation: each episode shows a new facet of a world or a type; the reader's map of the world grows.
- **Logic gates:** (1) each episode adds something no other episode adds (cut test: remove it and something is lost); (2) the sequence has a cumulative direction — the world, the protagonist or the reader's understanding changes across episodes even if nothing escalates; (3) the ending closes the accumulation (a summary image, a final type, a departure), not just the last episode.
- **Flags:** `EPISODE_INTERCHANGEABLE` — episodes can be swapped or removed without loss. `NO_ACCUMULATION` — the twentieth episode tells the reader nothing the third did not.
- **Examples:** *Don Quixote*; *Dead Souls*; *Lazarillo de Tormes*.

### Journey / quest
- **Recognize by:** movement through space toward (or away from) a place or object; stations along the road structure the work.
- **Engine:** the distance remaining plus what each station costs or teaches.
- **Logic gates:** (1) the stations differ in function, not only in scenery; (2) the traveller arriving is not the one who left — or the sameness is the point and is made visible; (3) the goal is reframed by the journey (the object matters less, or differently, than at the start).
- **Flags:** `SCENERY_STATIONS` — stations differ only in setting. `GOAL_UNTOUCHED` — the journey changes nothing about what the goal means.
- **Examples:** *The Odyssey*; *The Lord of the Rings*; *The Road*.

### Spiral
- **Recognize by:** the work keeps circling the same situation, compulsion or question, each turn tighter or deeper, until a final decision, collapse or breakthrough.
- **Engine:** narrowing: each pass costs more (money, health, trust, illusions) and leaves less room.
- **Logic gates:** (1) each pass differs measurably from the previous (what is lost, what is now impossible); (2) the circling has a cause inside the character or situation, not just authorial insistence; (3) the exit (decision, collapse) is prepared by the accumulated cost.
- **Flags:** `FLAT_CIRCLE` — passes repeat without tightening. `UNPAID_EXIT` — the final break comes without accumulated pressure.
- **Examples:** *The Gambler* (Dostoevsky); *Requiem for a Dream* (Selby).

### Loop / recurrence
- **Recognize by:** the same situation, day, life-pattern or role structure recurs: time loops, reincarnation, generational repetition, ritual repetition.
- **Engine:** difference within repetition. The reader watches for what changes, what is learned, what is carried over — and for the condition under which the loop could break.
- **Logic gates:**
  1. **Non-iterativity:** a recurrence that the reader can fully predict stops being an event. Each turn must contain something unrepeatable — a crossing that happens only once, a cost that cannot be undone, knowledge that changes the next turn.
  2. **Rules:** what carries over between turns (memory, guilt, traits, objects, relationships) is consistent, and the reader learns the rules early enough to read the variations.
  3. **Agency:** characters still make choices that matter; the mechanism of recurrence does not make choices for them.
  4. **Exit condition:** the work either breaks the loop for a cause prepared inside it, or makes the absence of exit the meaning — visibly, not by default.
- **Flags:** `ITERATED_NON_EVENT` — turns are predictable instances of the rule. `MECHANISM_CHOOSES` — role swaps or repeats happen automatically, and the cycle becomes an alibi that absolves characters of choice. `UNSTATED_CARRYOVER` — the reader cannot tell what passes between turns, so variations read as noise.
- **Examples:** *Groundhog Day* (film, 1993); *Life After Life* (Kate Atkinson); *Cloud Atlas* (David Mitchell; also nested).

### Fugue / refrain
- **Recognize by:** a motif, phrase, image or scene returns in new contexts and its meaning shifts each time; the work is organized by returns rather than by plot steps.
- **Engine:** re-contextualization: the reader re-reads the refrain against everything that happened since.
- **Logic gates:** (1) each return changes the refrain's meaning (from hope to irony, from care to control); (2) the contexts escalate or deepen in a direction; (3) the last return lands with the weight of all previous ones.
- **Flags:** `REFRAIN_UNCHANGED` — the motif returns with the same meaning, as decoration. `REFRAIN_OVERLOAD` — so many returns that the reader stops registering them.
- **Examples:** *Three Sisters* (Chekhov; "To Moscow!"); *Rashomon* (Kurosawa, 1950; one event re-told); "Todesfuge" (Celan).

### Mirror / chiasmus / ring
- **Recognize by:** the second half reflects the first (A-B-C-B'-A'), two characters or plots mirror each other, or the ending returns to the opening image or line.
- **Engine:** recognition: the reader anticipates the mirrored element and reads the difference.
- **Logic gates:** (1) the mirrored element differs meaningfully from its original (inversion, reversal of roles, changed cost); (2) the centre of the ring — the hinge — carries the heaviest event or revelation; (3) the return to the opening is earned: the reader sees the same image differently.
- **Flags:** `MIRROR_AS_COPY` — reflection without difference. `TRIP_AROUND_THE_BLOCK` — the ending returns to the start and nothing has moved (Baxter's warning about rhyming action; see lenses-literary). `WEAK_HINGE` — the centre of the symmetry is empty.
- **Examples:** *Wuthering Heights* (two generations); *The Master and Margarita* (Moscow / Yershalaim); *Finnegans Wake* (last sentence runs into the first).

### Frame & nested frames
- **Recognize by:** an outer situation (a telling, a confession, a fall, a deathbed, a manuscript found) contains the inner story; frames may nest several deep.
- **Engine:** two-way exchange: the inner story changes the meaning of the frame, and the frame situation changes how the inner story is told and heard.
- **Logic gates:**
  1. The frame has its own stakes: something is at risk in the telling situation itself (who listens, why now, what the telling will change).
  2. Exchange: at least one point where the inner story alters the frame (the teller changes, the listener acts) or the frame alters the inner story (a revision, a withheld fact surfaces because of the frame).
  3. The frame is not forgotten: it is either kept alive at intervals or its silence is deliberate and repaid at the end.
  4. For nested frames: each level adds a distinct perspective or reliability layer; levels close in reverse order of opening, or the failure to close is meaningful.
- **Flags:** `INERT_FRAME` — the frame is a mere device for delivering the story; nothing is at stake in it. `FRAME_FORGOTTEN` — the frame vanishes and returns only at the end with no repayment. `STACKED_NOT_NESTED` — levels sit side by side and do not inform each other.
- **Examples:** *Frankenstein*; *Heart of Darkness*; *A Hero of Our Time*.

### Analytic / retrospective
- **Recognize by:** the decisive events happened before the work begins; the present action is the uncovering of the past, and the uncovering is the catastrophe.
- **Engine:** disclosure: each revelation narrows the space of what can be denied.
- **Logic gates:** (1) the order of revelations is motivated by present pressure, not by authorial withholding; (2) each disclosure changes the present situation (relationships, decisions), not only the reader's knowledge; (3) the final disclosure recasts what the reader thought they knew.
- **Flags:** `ARBITRARY_WITHHOLDING` — facts are hidden only because the author needs them later. `INERT_PRESENT` — the present is a waiting room for flashbacks.
- **Examples:** *Oedipus Rex*; *Ghosts* (Ibsen).

### Radial / explosion
- **Recognize by:** one central event (often already known) is approached repeatedly from different angles, witnesses or times; the work orbits it.
- **Engine:** circumstance and meaning, not outcome: why it happened, who could have stopped it, what it reveals about each witness.
- **Logic gates:** (1) each approach reveals something the others do not; (2) the approaches together produce a picture no single one could; (3) the work's final angle changes the meaning of the event, not merely completes the inventory.
- **Flags:** `REDUNDANT_SPOKE` — an approach repeats known information. `NO_CENTRE_SHIFT` — the event means the same at the end as at the start.
- **Examples:** *Chronicle of a Death Foretold*; *Citizen Kane* (1941); *The Sweet Hereafter* (Russell Banks).

### Countdown / inevitability
- **Recognize by:** the ending is announced or known (title, prologue, genre, history); suspense moves from *what* to *how* and *why*.
- **Engine:** dramatic irony: the reader knows more than the characters and watches choices closing the door.
- **Logic gates:** (1) the known ending is used: scenes gain weight because of it; (2) there are moments where it could have gone otherwise, and the reader feels them; (3) the "how" or "why" delivers something the bare outcome did not.
- **Flags:** `DECORATIVE_FOREKNOWLEDGE` — the announced ending changes nothing in how scenes read. `NO_NEAR_MISS` — the path to the ending is a straight slide with no felt alternatives.
- **Examples:** *The Death of Ivan Ilyich*; *Romeo and Juliet* (prologue); *Chronicle of a Death Foretold*.

### Braided / multi-strand
- **Recognize by:** two or more storylines alternate, with separate protagonists, times or places; they may converge or only rhyme.
- **Engine:** cross-illumination and convergence: each strand comments on the others; cuts between strands create tension and irony.
- **Logic gates:** (1) each strand has its own movement and could carry interest alone; (2) the strands talk to each other — thematic rhyme, causal link or contrast — at specific, identifiable points; (3) the cut points are chosen (cliff, echo, contrast), not just alternating by rota; (4) if strands converge, the convergence is prepared.
- **Flags:** `PARALLEL_SILENCE` — strands never inform each other. `DEAD_STRAND` — one strand is markedly weaker and the reader skims it. `ROTA_CUTS` — alternation by schedule, not by meaning.
- **Examples:** *Anna Karenina* (Anna / Levin); *The Hours* (Cunningham); *Short Cuts* (Altman, 1993).

### Mosaic / network
- **Recognize by:** fragments, documents, voices or chapters the reader assembles; links are lateral, the order may be optional.
- **Engine:** pattern recognition: the reader's pleasure is in discovering connections the text does not state.
- **Logic gates:** (1) enough recurring nodes (names, objects, places, phrases) for connection to be possible; (2) the assembled whole means more than the sum; (3) the text trusts the reader without becoming opaque — at least one route through is legible.
- **Flags:** `UNCONNECTABLE` — too few shared nodes; fragments stay fragments. `OVEREXPLAINED_NETWORK` — connections are spelled out, removing the reader's work.
- **Examples:** *Hopscotch* (Cortázar); *Dictionary of the Khazars* (Pavić); *A Visit from the Goon Squad* (Egan).

### Reverse chronology
- **Recognize by:** events presented from late to early, wholly or in a dominant line.
- **Engine:** causal curiosity and irony: the reader knows the outcome and watches the causes appear; early innocence becomes painful.
- **Logic gates:** (1) each backward step reveals a cause or a lost possibility that changes the reading of what was already seen; (2) the earliest point (the work's end) has maximum charge; (3) the reversal is not a gimmick: forward telling would lose something specific.
- **Flags:** `GIMMICK_REVERSAL` — reorder to forward and nothing is lost. `FLAT_ORIGIN` — the last-shown (earliest) moment is weak.
- **Examples:** *Betrayal* (Pinter); *Time's Arrow* (Amis); *Memento* (Nolan; reverse main line).

### Fractal / mise en abyme
- **Recognize by:** a part replicates the whole at smaller scale (a play within the play, a chapter that is the book in miniature, a prologue whose pattern every section repeats).
- **Engine:** recognition across scales; the miniature predicts or comments on the whole.
- **Logic gates:** (1) the replica is recognizable but varied; (2) the replication does work: it foreshadows, provokes a character, or reframes the whole; (3) scales do not merely duplicate — something emerges at the large scale that the small does not contain.
- **Flags:** `ORNAMENTAL_REPLICA` — the miniature is clever but inert. `SCALE_DUPLICATE` — whole and part say the same thing.
- **Examples:** *Hamlet* ("The Mousetrap"); *The Counterfeiters* (Gide).

### Kishōtenketsu
- **Recognize by:** four movements: introduction, development, a turn that introduces an unexpected element or juxtaposition, and a reconciliation that makes the parts cohere. Conflict is optional.
- **Engine:** the turn (*ten*): a juxtaposition that reframes the first two movements.
- **Logic gates:** (1) the turn is genuinely new material, not a twist of the existing conflict; (2) the conclusion makes the juxtaposition meaningful; (3) the first two movements are complete enough to be reframed.
- **Flags:** `WEAK_TEN` — the turn is predictable or not reframing. `UNRECONCILED` — the conclusion does not tie the turn back.
- **Examples:** classical Chinese and Japanese four-line verse (origin of the form); four-panel *yonkoma* comics. See lenses-classical for detail.

### Stillness / situation
- **Recognize by:** little external action; the situation itself is the subject; change happens underneath (subtext, mood, relationships), often in what is not said.
- **Engine:** pressure of the unspoken and of time passing; the reader watches the undercurrent move while the surface stays still.
- **Logic gates:** (1) stillness differs from stasis: something shifts in each unit (a relationship, a self-understanding, a possibility closing); (2) subtext is legible — the reader can name what is not being said; (3) time is felt: the situation is worse, later or more final at the end.
- **Flags:** `STASIS` — nothing shifts underneath either. `ILLEGIBLE_SUBTEXT` — the reader cannot infer the undercurrent.
- **Examples:** *The Cherry Orchard*; *Waiting for Godot*; *Oblomov*.

## Borrowed forms

Forms from music, dance and ritual used as compositional models, often signalled by part titles.

### Title-as-architecture test (apply to every borrowed form)
- Each part must perform the **function** of its formal slot, not just carry its name. An adagio slows and binds; a coda recaps and climaxes; a recapitulation returns material transformed.
- Across the sequence, titles should deepen or counterpoint the content. If the titles could be swapped or removed without loss, they are **wallpaper**, not architecture.
- Ask the author which properties of the form they are borrowing (tempo, number of voices, return, ritual order); test those, not the whole musicological apparatus.
- Flags: `TITLE_AS_WALLPAPER` — the form is named but not enacted. `SLOT_MISFIT` — a part does the opposite of its slot's function without the inversion being meaningful.

### Sonata form
- **Recognize by:** exposition of two contrasting themes (often in tension), development that fragments and combines them, recapitulation that brings both back transformed or reconciled; optional coda.
- **Slot functions:** exposition establishes the opposition; development puts it under stress; recapitulation returns with the opposition changed.
- **Logic gates:** (1) two themes are genuinely in opposition; (2) development transforms rather than repeats; (3) the return shows what the development did.
- **Flags:** `NO_SECOND_THEME`, `RECAP_UNCHANGED`.
- **Examples:** *Tonio Kröger* (Mann) is often described as sonata-like (attribution uncertain).

### Theme and variations
- **Recognize by:** one theme stated, then reworked repeatedly in different manners, keys or perspectives.
- **Slot functions:** the theme must be clear enough to recognize; each variation explores a different property of it.
- **Logic gates:** (1) the theme is recognizable in each variation; (2) each variation reveals a new aspect; (3) the order of variations has a direction (intensification, darkening, return).
- **Flags:** `THEME_LOST`, `VARIATION_DUPLICATE`.
- **Examples:** *Exercises in Style* (Queneau); *The Book of Laughter and Forgetting* (Kundera described it as a novel in the form of variations).

### Rondo
- **Recognize by:** a recurring section alternating with contrasting episodes (A-B-A-C-A).
- **Slot functions:** A is home; episodes are departures; each return to A is coloured by the episode just heard.
- **Logic gates:** (1) episodes contrast with A and with each other; (2) returns to A feel different after each episode.
- **Flags:** `HOME_NEVER_CHANGES`.
- **Examples:** no canonical literary example asserted; test function, not name.

### Fugue (musical)
- **Recognize by:** a subject stated by one voice, answered by others in turn, overlapping; episodes between entries; often a stretto where entries crowd together near the end.
- **Slot functions:** each voice takes up the same subject in its own register; density increases toward the end.
- **Logic gates:** (1) the "voices" (characters, narrators, timelines) are distinct; (2) all of them carry the same subject recognizably; (3) entries accelerate or overlap toward the end.
- **Flags:** `VOICES_MERGE`, `NO_STRETTO`.
- **Examples:** "Sirens" episode of *Ulysses* (Joyce's own schema names a fugue technique); "Todesfuge" (Celan).

### Symphony movements
- **Recognize by:** a multi-part work whose parts contrast in tempo and character (typically fast, slow, dance-like, finale).
- **Slot functions:** the opening sets the conflict, the slow movement deepens feeling, the dance-like movement relieves or distorts, the finale resolves or overturns.
- **Logic gates:** (1) parts differ in tempo and texture on the page (scene length, sentence rhythm, density of event); (2) the finale relates to the first movement.
- **Flags:** `FLAT_TEMPO`.
- **Examples:** *Napoleon Symphony* (Burgess; modelled on Beethoven's *Eroica*).

### Suite
- **Recognize by:** a sequence of contrasting pieces in one key or mood world, without development between them.
- **Slot functions:** each piece is complete; unity comes from shared material, not progression.
- **Logic gates:** (1) each piece is self-sufficient; (2) shared material is felt; (3) the order creates a pleasing contrast curve.
- **Flags:** `SUITE_EXCUSE` — "it's a suite" used to cover missing connections the work actually needs.
- **Examples:** no canonical literary example asserted.

### Grand pas de deux
- **Recognize by:** a five-part partnered set piece: entrée (entrance, the couple presents itself), adagio (slow partnered dancing, one supports, lifts and balances the other), variation of one partner (solo), variation of the other (solo), coda (both together, fastest and most brilliant, recapping and climaxing).
- **Slot functions:** entrée introduces the pair; adagio shows dependence and support — who holds whom; variations show each alone, what each is without the other; coda reunites at maximum energy.
- **Logic gates:** (1) the "pair" is clear (two characters, two forces, two lives); (2) the adagio part shows the partnership as mutual support or dependence, not just co-presence; (3) the solo parts show each partner apart and different; (4) the coda returns them together at a higher pitch.
- **Flags:** `NO_SUPPORT_IN_ADAGIO`, `SOLOS_INDISTINCT`, `CODA_DECRESCENDO` — a deliberate fading coda must be shown to be deliberate.
- **Examples:** the Act III wedding pas de deux in *The Sleeping Beauty*; the Black Swan pas de deux in *Swan Lake*.

### Ballet acts: pas d'action vs. divertissement
- **Recognize by:** narrative ballet alternates dances that advance the story (pas d'action, mime scenes) with display dances that pause it (divertissements: national dances, character dances, celebrations).
- **Slot functions:** pas d'action moves plot and relationship; divertissement offers pleasure, texture, world, rest; a "white act" (ballet blanc) moves into another reality.
- **Logic gates:** (1) the author knows which chapters/scenes are action and which are display; (2) display units earn their place by texture, contrast or world-building; (3) the proportion matches intent.
- **Flags:** `DIVERTISSEMENT_SPRAWL` — display units crowd out action. `UNACKNOWLEDGED_PAUSE` — a pause is presented as if it advanced the plot.
- **Examples:** *The Nutcracker* Act II (Kingdom of Sweets divertissement); *Giselle* Act II and *Swan Lake* Act II (white acts).

### Rite of passage / liturgy
- **Recognize by:** a ritual sequence structures the work: separation, liminality (threshold, testing, suspension of normal rules), incorporation (return in a new status) — van Gennep's three phases; or a fixed liturgical order (mass, requiem, funeral rite).
- **Slot functions:** separation strips the old status; the liminal phase tests and transforms; incorporation confirms the new status — or fails to.
- **Logic gates:** (1) the threshold is marked and costly; (2) the liminal phase suspends ordinary rules visibly; (3) incorporation (or its failure) is the point of the ending.
- **Flags:** `UNMARKED_THRESHOLD`, `NO_INCORPORATION` — failed incorporation must read as failure, not as omission.
- **Examples:** *Requiem* (Akhmatova; the cycle borrows the name of the liturgical form — how far it follows the rite is for the reader to test).

### Calendar / cycle of seasons
- **Recognize by:** the work is organized by days, months, feasts or seasons; time is cyclic and the cycle carries meaning (birth, growth, decline, death, renewal). Frye mapped comedy, romance, tragedy and satire onto spring, summer, autumn and winter (*Anatomy of Criticism*, 1957).
- **Slot functions:** each station of the year brings its own mood and expected events; deviations from the expected season mood are meaningful.
- **Logic gates:** (1) each station contributes something specific beyond the date; (2) the cycle's return is marked by change (the second spring is not the first).
- **Flags:** `DATE_LABELS_ONLY` — calendar used as chapter labels only.
- **Examples:** *The Shepheardes Calender* (Spenser); *Лето Господне* (Shmelyov; the church year).
