# Classical drama & non-Western form lenses

Load when: checking unity, causality, reversal and recognition; testing the moral engine or the premise; using a pyramid at act or scene level; or when a work turns through juxtaposition or tempo rather than conflict.

## Index
- **Unity, probability, reversal, recognition** (Aristotle) — removability test, causal necessity, the hinge of the action.
- **Collision of justified powers** (Hegel) — moral conflict where both sides have right on their side.
- **Premise and conflict** (Egri) — premise as a testable claim; static, jumping and rising conflict.
- **Pyramid and its moments** (Freytag) — ignition, peak and turn inside an act, chapter or sequence.
- **Ki-shō-ten-ketsu** — four-part form that turns by juxtaposition, not conflict.
- **Jo-ha-kyū** (Zeami) — tempo law: slow, breaking, rapid, at every scale.

### Unity, probability, reversal, recognition — Aristotle, *Poetics* (c. 335 BCE)
- **Core:** Plot (mythos), the arrangement of incidents, comes first. A whole has a beginning, middle and end; its parts are so connected that moving or removing one disjoints the whole. Events should follow by probability or necessity, not merely one after another; episodic plots are the worst. Complex plots turn through reversal (action veers into its opposite) and recognition (ignorance to knowledge), best when they coincide; the best recognition arises from the incidents themselves, the least artistic from tokens and signs. Hamartia is an error or misjudgement, not necessarily a moral flaw.
- **Ask (plan):** Remove each major section, life or act in thought: does the whole fall apart? If not, why is it there? Does each event follow from the previous by probability or necessity? Where is the reversal and where the recognition, and do they coincide? What is the protagonist's error: misjudgement, ignorance, or vice?
- **Ask (text):** Quote the hinge where the reversal happens: is it caused from within the action? Quote the recognition: what exactly does the character now know, and what in the incidents led there?
- **Flags:** `EPISODIC` — sections linked only by sequence or theme; removable without loss. `EXTERNAL_RESOLUTION` — the outcome does not arise from the plot itself (deus ex machina). `RECOGNITION_BY_TOKEN` — knowledge delivered by a sign, document or messenger instead of the incidents. `FLAW_AS_VICE` — reading hamartia as a moral flaw and moralizing the character.
- **Use when:** any work that makes tight causal claims; drama; testing episodic or cyclic structures.
- **Set aside when:** deliberately episodic forms (picaresque, chronicle, linked stories) — then ask what replaces unity of action (theme, pattern, voice). The unities of time and place are later neoclassical additions, not Aristotle's demand.
- **RU terms:** фабула (мифос), единство действия, вероятность и необходимость, перипетия, узнавание, ошибка (гамартия), эпизодическая фабула, страдание (пафос).

### Collision of justified powers — G. W. F. Hegel, *Lectures on Aesthetics* (delivered 1820s; publ. 1835–38)
- **Core:** Tragic conflict is a collision of two ethical powers, each justified and each one-sided in asserting itself against the other; his central example is Antigone (family against state). The resolution restores the unity that the one-sidedness broke. The Russian theoretical term «коллизия» descends from this usage.
- **Ask (plan):** State the collision: which two goods or rights clash? Is each side genuinely justified, or is one a strawman? Who embodies each side, and do they ever exchange positions? What does the resolution restore, or fail to restore?
- **Ask (text):** Quote the strongest justification of each side in the characters' own terms.
- **Flags:** `GOOD_VS_EVIL` — the collision collapses into right against wrong. `ONE_SIDED_RIGHT` — only one side is articulated.
- **Use when:** tragedy, moral and political drama, perpetrator-and-victim material.
- **Set aside when:** comedy of manners and light genre — keep only as a quick check.
- **RU terms:** коллизия, нравственные силы, односторонность, трагическое, примирение.

### Premise and conflict — Lajos Egri, *The Art of Dramatic Writing* (1946)
- **Core:** A good play rests on a clearly formulated premise: a compact claim that implies character, conflict and outcome (his example for *Romeo and Juliet*: great love defies even death). Characters grow through conflict. Conflict can be static (no movement), jumping (leaps without the intermediate steps) or rising (steady escalation), and foreshadowing conflict prepares it. Opponents are bound by a unity of opposites, so neither can simply walk away.
- **Ask (plan):** Can the premise be stated as a claim the work tests — proves, disproves or leaves open? What binds the opponents so that leaving would cost too much? Where does the conflict stall, and where does it jump?
- **Ask (text):** Quote a transition where a character changes stance: are the intermediate steps on the page? Quote what binds the opponents.
- **Flags:** `STATIC_CONFLICT` — the same exchange repeated without movement. `JUMPING_CONFLICT` — a change of stance with the steps missing. `EASY_EXIT` — opponents could part without cost. `THESIS_DRIVEN` — the work reduced to proving a moral, characters reduced to arguments.
- **Use when:** drama, screenplays, two-handers; stalled character change.
- **Set aside when:** ambiguity is the goal — use the premise only as the question the work asks, not a claim it proves.
- **RU terms:** Russian translations of the terms vary — use: посылка (главная мысль), статичный / скачкообразный / нарастающий конфликт, предвестие конфликта, единство противоположностей.

### Pyramid and its moments — Gustav Freytag, *Die Technik des Dramas* (1863)
- **Core:** Five parts of a (chiefly five-act, tragic) drama: introduction, rise, climax, return or fall, catastrophe; and three moments: the exciting moment (rising action begins), the tragic moment (the fall begins), the moment of final suspense (a last delay before the catastrophe). Built from Greek tragedy and Shakespeare, not a universal law.
- **Ask (plan):** Use it on an act, chapter or sequence, not on the whole work: does the unit have an ignition, a peak and a turn? Is there a moment of final suspense before the catastrophe?
- **Ask (text):** Quote the exciting moment of a chapter or act; quote the peak and the first sign of the turn.
- **Flags:** `NO_IGNITION` — a unit that never starts. `PYRAMID_IMPOSED` — a critique that demands a pyramid from a work with another design.
- **Use when:** tragic or five-act drama; a single sequence that sags.
- **Set aside when:** as a whole-work template for novels or non-linear works — it misdiagnoses them; commercial beat sheets inherit this bias.
- **RU terms:** экспозиция (введение), завязка (возбуждающий момент), развитие (подъём), кульминация, поворот (спад), катастрофа (развязка).

### Ki-shō-ten-ketsu — Chinese qǐ-chéng-zhuǎn-hé, adopted in Japan (classical poetry; four-panel comics)
- **Core:** Four parts: ki (introduction), shō (development), ten (a turn: a new, seemingly unrelated element or a juxtaposition that reframes what came before), ketsu (conclusion that makes the reader hold the parts together). Conflict is not the engine; the turn reframes rather than escalates. It works at the scale of a poem, a scene, a chapter or a whole work.
- **Ask (plan):** Where is the ten in each unit, and what new element reframes the first two parts? Does the ketsu make the reader hold both together? Is a unit being forced into conflict when juxtaposition is its real engine?
- **Ask (text):** Quote the ten: does it change the meaning of the first half, or only add to it?
- **Flags:** `WEAK_TEN` — the turn adds information but does not reframe. `KETSU_EXPLAINS` — the conclusion explains the juxtaposition instead of letting it resonate.
- **Use when:** quiet, lyrical, linked or observational works; chapters accused of having "no conflict".
- **Set aside when:** the work is driven by opposed wills — use conflict lenses instead.
- **RU terms:** no established Russian terms — use: вступление, развитие, поворот, заключение (ки-сё-тэн-кэцу).

### Jo-ha-kyū — Zeami Motokiyo, treatises on noh (early 15th c.)
- **Core:** A tempo law: jo (slow, introductory), ha (breaking open, development, quickening), kyū (rapid, concluding). Zeami applies it at every scale: a day's programme, a play, a section, a single dance or phrase. It concerns acceleration and release, not conflict.
- **Ask (plan):** Does the whole work quicken from jo to kyū? Does each chapter, act or episode? Where does a unit stay at one tempo throughout? Is the kyū brief and decisive, or drawn out?
- **Ask (text):** Quote the opening and closing passages of a unit and compare sentence length, scene length and density of events.
- **Flags:** `FLAT_TEMPO` — no acceleration anywhere in the unit. `LATE_JO` — slow exposition returning in the final stretch. `DRAGGED_KYU` — the rapid close stretched out.
- **Use when:** pacing; performance forms; works structured on music or dance; film and series.
- **Set aside when:** a structure decelerates by design — then check that the deceleration is controlled.
- **RU terms:** дзё-ха-кю: медленно — ломка — быстро.

## Combining
- Aristotle gives the causal test, Hegel the moral engine: use both on tragedy and on perpetrator-and-victim material.
- Egri's premise pushes toward a thesis, Hegel's collision toward two justified sides: run Hegel to check the premise has not made one side a strawman.
- Freytag conflicts with ki-shō-ten-ketsu and jo-ha-kyū at whole-work scale: choose one shape per unit.
- Jo-ha-kyū pairs with M. Chekhov's polarity (lenses-russian.md) and Genette's duration (lenses-literary.md) for tempo diagnosis.
- If Aristotle's removability test finds the work episodic, ask which Alison pattern (lenses-literary.md) unifies it before calling it a fault.
