# Literary craft & narrative theory lenses

Load when: the work is literary, non-linear, pattern-driven, quiet, or heavy on voice and point of view; or when a commercial lens would misdiagnose it.

## Index
- **Narrative patterns** (Alison) — non-arc shapes: spiral, meander, explosion, network, fractal.
- **Narrative discourse** (Genette) — order, speed, frequency, focalization, narrating voice; frames and retrospection.
- **Story, plot, pattern, rhythm** (Forster) — causality vs. sequence; flat and round characters; repetition with variation.
- **Desire for the end** (Brooks) — endings, retrospective narration, repetition as working-through.
- **Responsibility and rhyme** (Baxter) — agency, rhyming scenes, counterpoint, stillness, epiphany endings.
- **Scene and picture** (Lubbock, after James) — summary vs. dramatized scene; the reflecting consciousness.
- **Free indirect style** (Wood) — the blend of narrator and character; irony of distance.
- **Crowding and leaping** (Le Guin) — what to dwell on and what to skip; repetition; POV discipline.
- **Continuous dream, psychic distance** (Gardner) — immersion and its intended or accidental breaks.
- **Designing principle, moral argument, symbol web** (Truby) — the form's logic, theme through action, symbol accrual.
- **Variations** (Kundera) — multi-part novels built as variations on a theme; tempo of parts.

### Narrative patterns — Jane Alison, *Meander, Spiral, Explode: Design and Pattern in Narrative* (2019)
- **Core:** Momentum need not come from a rising and falling arc. Alison names patterns from nature: wavelets (small rises and falls), meander (a winding path between points), spiral (circling back while moving, toward a decision or a deepening), radial/explosion (everything radiates from one central event, often already past), network/cells (discrete units the reader connects), fractal (each part repeats the shape of the whole at a smaller scale). Pattern is made by repetition, symmetry, viewpoint and imagery.
- **Ask (plan):** Which pattern does the work actually use, and does the author know it? If spiral: what deepens on each pass, and where does it converge? If radial: what is the center, and does every section point toward it? If fractal: does each section repeat the whole's shape, and does the change of scale mean something? What produces forward pull if not escalation?
- **Ask (text):** Quote the recurring elements that build the pattern (images, phrases, situations) and show the variation between occurrences. Quote section openings and endings to locate the pattern's turning points.
- **Flags:** `PATTERN_UNDECLARED` — the work abandons the arc but adopts no pattern, so it reads as shapeless. `SPIRAL_WITHOUT_DEPTH` — returns without accumulation: a circle, not a spiral. `DIFFUSE_CENTER` — a radial structure whose center is vague. `ARC_RELAPSE` — a patterned work that snaps into a conventional climax at the end, against its own design.
- **Use when:** non-linear, cyclic, mosaic, fragmentary or linked-story works; whenever an arc-based critique feels wrong.
- **Set aside when:** the work is plainly arc-driven; never use pattern as an excuse for missing momentum.
- **RU terms:** no verified Russian edition — use: волна, меандр, спираль, взрыв (радиальная структура), сеть (ячейки), фрактал.

### Narrative discourse — Gérard Genette, «Discours du récit» (in *Figures III*, 1972)
- **Core:** Separates story (what happens) from narrative (the text). Order: analepsis, prolepsis, anachrony. Duration: pause, scene, summary, ellipsis — the speed of telling. Frequency: singulative (told once, happened once), repetitive (one event told many times), iterative (many events told once: "every Sunday"). Mood: distance and focalization (zero, internal, external). Voice: who narrates, from when, and from which level (frame or embedded story).
- **Ask (plan):** Map each section's anachronies: from which time is it told, and which time does it reach? Is the narrating instance (where and when the telling happens) defined, and does it matter to what is told? Where is frequency a design choice? Who focalizes each section?
- **Ask (text):** Quote shifts in time and check that the reader can locate them. Quote changes of speed: where is a key event summarized when it needed a scene, or a minor one stretched? Quote focalization leaks: knowledge the focal character could not have.
- **Flags:** `UNANCHORED_ANACHRONY` — the reader cannot tell when they are. `SPEED_MISMATCH` — the weight of an event is inverse to the space it gets. `POV_LEAK` — knowledge beyond the focal character. `INERT_FRAME` — the narrating situation is established but never affects what or how it tells.
- **Use when:** frames, retrospective narrators, non-linear time, several POVs, ritual or recurring material.
- **Set aside when:** rarely; use lightly for simple linear works.
- **RU terms:** аналепсис, пролепсис, анахрония, сцена, резюме, пауза, эллипсис, сингулятивный / повторяющий / итеративный рассказ, фокализация, нарратор, время повествования.

### Story, plot, pattern, rhythm — E. M. Forster, *Aspects of the Novel* (1927)
- **Core:** A story is events in time sequence ("and then"); a plot adds causality ("because": the queen died of grief). Flat characters are built around a single idea; round ones can surprise in a convincing way. In "Pattern and Rhythm", pattern is the overall shape (his examples include an hourglass) and rhythm is repetition with variation of an element that gathers meaning (his example: a recurring musical phrase in Proust). He warned that a rigid pattern can cost a novel its life.
- **Ask (plan):** Where does "and then" replace "because"? Which characters are flat by design and which by neglect? Is there an overall pattern, and does it bend characters to fit it? Which elements recur as rhythm?
- **Ask (text):** Quote transitions between major events: is the causal link on the page? Quote the moment a supposedly round character surprises: convincing or arbitrary? Quote each return of a rhythmic element: does it grow?
- **Flags:** `AND_THEN_CHAIN` — sequence without causality. `ACCIDENTALLY_FLAT` — a major character reducible to one trait. `PATTERN_STRANGLES_LIFE` — characters bent to fit the shape. `RHYTHM_WITHOUT_GROWTH` — a repeated element that gathers nothing.
- **Use when:** a quick universal check of causality, character and rhythm.
- **Set aside when:** picaresque or chronicle forms where "and then" is the design — check rhythm instead.
- **RU terms:** история / сюжет (Forster's story / plot; Russian translations vary), плоский / объёмный персонаж, узор, ритм.

### Desire for the end — Peter Brooks, *Reading for the Plot: Design and Intention in Narrative* (1984)
- **Core:** Reading is driven by desire for the ending, which gives meaning back to what came before: we read in anticipation of retrospection. In "Freud's Masterplot", repetitions in the middle bind energy and delay the end; plot is a detour that must neither end too soon (short circuit) nor wander indefinitely. Repetition can be working-through or compulsion.
- **Ask (plan):** What ending does the opening make the reader want? Does the ending fulfil, reverse or reframe that desire? Is the middle a meaningful detour or a holding pattern? Do repetitions move toward understanding, or recur compulsively — and is compulsion the point?
- **Ask (text):** Quote the opening's signals of the end. Quote the ending, then reread three middle passages through it: do they gain meaning? Quote repeated situations: what is different the last time?
- **Flags:** `SHORT_CIRCUIT` — the end arrives before the middle has earned it. `ENDLESS_MIDDLE` — detour without binding. `ENDING_DOES_NOT_RETROSPECT` — the ending adds nothing to the meaning of earlier parts.
- **Use when:** retrospective narrators, frames, mysteries, trauma and repetition structures.
- **Set aside when:** rarely.
- **RU terms:** no verified Russian edition — use: желание конца, предвосхищение ретроспекции, повторение, проработка, короткое замыкание.

### Responsibility and rhyme — Charles Baxter, *Burning Down the House: Essays on Fiction* (1997; expanded 2008)
- **Core:** "Dysfunctional Narratives" criticizes stories in which nobody chooses or is accountable: things happen to characters, in the passive voice of "mistakes were made". "Rhyming Action" treats echoes between scenes as rhymes: related, not identical, so each deepens the other. "Counterpointed Characterization" sets a character's surface against an opposed undercurrent. "Stillness" defends moments where action stops and attention deepens; "Against Epiphanies" warns against the obligatory revelation ending. *The Art of Subtext* (2007) adds the "request moment", a scene that turns on what one character asks of another (attribution uncertain).
- **Ask (plan):** Who makes the decisive choices, and who is accountable for them? Is a force (fate, system, trauma, history, karma) doing the choosing for the characters? Which scenes are designed to rhyme with which? Does the ending rest on an insight rather than an action?
- **Ask (text):** Quote the decisive moments: who is the grammatical and dramatic agent? Quote paired scenes: identical, or rhyming? Quote still passages: what fills the stillness?
- **Flags:** `DYSFUNCTIONAL_NARRATIVE` — harm with no agent; characters only acted upon. `ECHO_NOT_RHYME` — repetition without variation. `EPIPHANY_BY_DEFAULT` — an insight replaces action at the end. `STASIS_NOT_STILLNESS` — a pause without heightened attention.
- **Use when:** victim narratives, fate or system narratives, literary fiction, linked stories.
- **Set aside when:** powerlessness is the subject — then ask whether it is dramatized or merely asserted.
- **RU terms:** no verified Russian edition — use: дисфункциональный нарратив, рифмующееся действие, контрапункт характера, неподвижность, эпифания.

### Scene and picture — Percy Lubbock, *The Craft of Fiction* (1921), after Henry James
- **Core:** Lubbock contrasts picture (panoramic summary, the narrator's survey over time) with scene (the dramatized moment), and holds that questions of method turn on point of view. From James's practice comes the reflecting consciousness: a centre through which events are filtered.
- **Ask (plan):** For each section, what is scene and what is picture, and is the balance deliberate? Whose consciousness filters each section, and what does that filter hide or distort?
- **Ask (text):** Quote a key event: scene or picture? Quote where the reflector misreads, and whether the text lets the reader see past them.
- **Flags:** `SUMMARY_WHERE_SCENE_NEEDED`, `SCENE_INFLATION` — minor moments dramatized at length, `REFLECTOR_TOO_WISE` — the filter knows what it should not.
- **Use when:** POV and pacing questions in realist and psychological fiction.
- **Set aside when:** Genette is already in use for the same questions; choose one.
- **RU terms:** сцена, картина (панорама), точка зрения, центр сознания (рефлектор).

### Free indirect style — James Wood, *How Fiction Works* (2008)
- **Core:** Free indirect style lets third-person narration take on a character's words and perspective without quotation marks, so narrator and character blend; irony comes from the gap between them. Wood treats it as central to the modern novel.
- **Ask (plan):** Whose idiom colours the narration of each section? Is the narrator's distance from each character a planned variable?
- **Ask (text):** Quote sentences where the narration borrows a character's words: is the boundary controlled? Quote places where one character's idiom bleeds into another's section. Quote irony that depends on the gap.
- **Flags:** `IDIOM_BLEED` — one character's diction contaminating another's section unintentionally. `UNIRONIC_BORROWING` — narration adopts a character's view uncritically where the work needs distance.
- **Use when:** close third person; morally compromised or unreliable focal characters.
- **Set aside when:** first-person or strictly external narration.
- **RU terms:** несобственно-прямая речь.

### Crowding and leaping — Ursula K. Le Guin, *Steering the Craft* (1998; rev. 2015)
- **Core:** Le Guin's exercises cover the sound of prose, sentence length, repetition as a deliberate structural tool, point of view and changing it, indirect narration, and "crowding and leaping": what to fill densely and what to jump over so the story keeps its trajectory.
- **Ask (plan):** In each section, what is crowded (given room) and what is leapt over? Do the leaps land where the reader can follow? Are POV changes planned per section, or do they drift?
- **Ask (text):** Quote a leap: does the reader land? Quote crowded passages: do they serve the trajectory? Quote unplanned POV shifts inside a scene.
- **Flags:** `CROWDED_TANGENT` — density spent off the trajectory. `LEAP_TOO_FAR` — the reader loses the thread. `POV_DRIFT` — unplanned shifts of viewpoint.
- **Use when:** pacing, compression, POV discipline, deliberate repetition.
- **Set aside when:** working only from a plan — this lens needs text.
- **RU terms:** no standard Russian terms — use: наполнять и перепрыгивать, точка зрения, повтор.

### Continuous dream, psychic distance — John Gardner, *The Art of Fiction* (1983)
- **Core:** Fiction should create a vivid and continuous dream in the reader's mind; whatever jolts the reader out of it (confusion, clumsy prose, needless self-consciousness) is a fault unless intended. Gardner's psychic-distance scale runs from a distant, external view of a figure to deep inside their mind; abrupt shifts break the dream. "Profluence", the sense that the narrative is going somewhere, is commonly glossed from his work (attribution uncertain).
- **Ask (plan):** Where does the design break the dream on purpose (frames, address to the reader, fragmentation), and what does each break buy? What produces forward movement in each section?
- **Ask (text):** Quote abrupt changes of psychic distance: intended? Quote passages that pull the reader out by confusion about who, where or when.
- **Flags:** `UNINTENDED_JOLT`, `DISTANCE_LURCH`, `NO_PROFLUENCE`.
- **Use when:** immersion-based fiction; frame returns; POV control.
- **Set aside when:** deliberately anti-illusionist works (Brechtian theatre, metafiction) — then judge whether breaks are controlled, not whether they exist.
- **RU terms:** no verified Russian edition — use: непрерывный сон, психологическая дистанция, поступательность.

### Designing principle, moral argument, symbol web — John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story* (2007)
- **Core:** The premise is the concrete "what"; the designing principle is the abstract "how": the organizing process or form that makes this telling distinct. The moral argument makes theme emerge from characters' actions and their consequences, with strong opponents holding real values, instead of being stated. The symbol web attaches symbols to characters, places and objects so they accumulate and shift meaning. The story world expresses the characters' situation.
- **Ask (plan):** State the designing principle in one sentence: does every section carry it out? What moral positions do the main opponents hold, and is each made strong? Map the main symbols to characters and places: does their meaning shift across the work?
- **Ask (text):** Quote actions that carry the moral argument vs. lines that merely state it. Quote successive occurrences of a key symbol and track the shift.
- **Flags:** `PRINCIPLE_INCONSISTENT` — a distinctive form whose logic is not kept. `THEME_AS_SPEECH` — the moral argument is voiced instead of enacted. `STATIC_SYMBOL` — a symbol that means the same thing every time.
- **Use when:** theme, motif and structural design; developing a premise.
- **Set aside when:** tempted to use his seven or twenty-two steps as a template — they assume a single protagonist with weakness, need, desire and self-revelation; for ensemble, cyclic or non-protagonist works use only the parts above.
- **RU terms:** Russian edition terms not verified — use: принцип построения, моральный аргумент, символическая сеть, мир истории.

### Variations — Milan Kundera, *The Art of the Novel* (1986)
- **Core:** Kundera describes some of his novels as variations on a theme rather than a single line of plot, and uses "polyphony" (in a sense different from Bakhtin's) for the simultaneous development of several lines — narrative, essayistic, dreamlike — held together by a common theme. He treats composition as deliberate: the number and proportion of parts and the tempo of each.
- **Ask (plan):** If the work is a set of variations, what is the theme, and what does each variation reveal that the others do not? Are the tempos of the parts differentiated by design? Are essayistic or dream sections joined by theme, or just interleaved?
- **Ask (text):** Quote the thematic word or situation in each part: does its meaning shift?
- **Flags:** `REDUNDANT_VARIATION` — a part that only restates another. `FLAT_TEMPO` — all parts move at the same speed.
- **Use when:** multi-part, essayistic or mosaic novels.
- **Set aside when:** single-line narratives.
- **RU terms:** вариации, полифония (в смысле Кундеры), композиция, темп части.

## Combining
- Alison names the shape; Genette measures order, speed and frequency inside it. Use them together on any non-linear work.
- Brooks pairs with Shklovsky and Vygotsky (lenses-russian.md) on endings and order; Baxter pairs with Lotman: an event needs both a crossing and an agent.
- Truby's moral argument and Bakhtin's polyphony both demand strong opponents, but Truby's single designing principle can pull toward monologism.
- Gardner's continuous dream conflicts with Shklovsky's estrangement and with frame-heavy designs: decide which governs before diagnosing breaks.
- Forster's rhythm, Kundera's variations and M. Chekhov's repetition (lenses-russian.md) form one toolkit for recurrence.
